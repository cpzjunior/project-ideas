# The Anti-Mage: The Story

## Introduction

Who watches over those who have the power to govern an entire society? And what happens when someone acquires enough power to judge them without having to answer for their own decisions?

Alden's story begins in a society where magic determines not only individual abilities but also access to power, influence, and opportunity. In Veridion, mages represent approximately ten percent of the population, yet they control most positions of authority and prestige. The rest, despite constituting the majority, do not enjoy the same rights and opportunities. This structure does not depend exclusively on force or explicitly discriminatory laws. It is also sustained by institutions, traditions, and relationships of dependence that normalize the superiority of those born with magic.

Alden belongs to the portion of the population subjected to this system. His journey, however, is not limited to overcoming the circumstances into which he was born. By gaining access to magic and entering the institutions he had always observed from the outside, he discovers that power is not necessarily associated with competence, responsibility, or justice. Later, after encountering a creature from another reality and accepting a pact that grants him anti-magic, he acquires an ability that disrupts precisely one of the main mechanisms of control within magical society: the ability to identify the traces left behind by a caster's actions.

What begins as a survival strategy gradually becomes an investigation into how power operates. Alden learns to recognize institutional weaknesses, gets closer to those who control them, and discovers how to use their own structures to achieve results that would otherwise remain beyond his reach. This experience, however, produces a growing contradiction. The more effective he becomes at fighting corruption, the more convinced he is that he can decide for himself when rules should be followed and when they can be ignored.

His rise to the position of Archmage represents the point at which this contradiction ceases to be merely personal and begins to involve the fate of society itself. Alden finally acquires the authority he spent his life questioning, but must confront the fact that his journey to the top was built, in part, on violating the very principles of accountability he is now expected to uphold.

## The Boy from the Countryside

Alden was a citizen of Veridion. At least, that was what the records said. For those without magic, however, this status meant very little. Non-mages lived under the influence of the magical elite and did not enjoy the same opportunities for participation, development, and social advancement. His family worked for one of the great arcane families, on land that did not belong to them and under conditions determined by people with the authority to decide their future. It was in this environment that Alden grew up, understanding from an early age that effort and freedom were not necessarily rewarded in the same way.

The countryside provided him with an education that would later prove just as important as any academic instruction. Hunting was part of the family's routine, and Alden learned to recognize tracks, follow trails, observe the wind, and remain silent until the right moment to act. He developed skill with the bow and later with the spear. More than survival techniques, these experiences taught him to assess risks, manage resources, and understand that force is not always the best solution. A mistake could mean losing the hunt, wasting resources, or putting someone in danger. These lessons would stay with him long after he left the countryside.

His perception of social relationships also developed during this period. Mages represented only a small portion of the population, yet they had shaped the institutions, professions, academies, hierarchies, and very notion of prestige. Alden did not need to understand Veridion's political structure to recognize inequality. He only had to observe his family, the decisions made about their work, and the ease with which people of humble origins were treated as inferior. Contempt rarely needed to be expressed openly. It appeared in the way others spoke, in the privileges they considered natural, and in the absence of any expectation that non-mages could challenge their circumstances.

This did not lead him to regard all mages as enemies. Alden recognized that some protected cities, saved lives, and treated non-mages with genuine respect. His distrust was less related to people's individual nature than to the structure that determined who was born with authority and who would spend their life depending on it. The kindness of certain individuals did not change the fact that one segment of society concentrated power without necessarily being subject to equivalent mechanisms of accountability.

When his magic finally awakened, years later than it normally would, Alden received an opportunity his family had never had. The event did not erase his origins or automatically grant him the prestige enjoyed by the old bloodlines, but it opened a concrete possibility of changing his own fate. For the first time, he could leave behind the position of those who served the elite and attempt to earn a place among those who belonged to it.

## Training and Admission to the Academy

Awakening magic did not mean knowing how to use it. In Veridion, mages were trained from childhood in a discipline combining study, practice, and martial preparation. Mastering magical energy required years of development, control, and repetition. Alden had missed that formative period and needed to make up for a considerable gap compared to those who had grown up within magical institutions. Furthermore, the academies were expensive, and his family did not have the resources to finance his education.

While looking for an alternative, he decided to return to the roads and work as an adventurer. He explored dungeons, fought creatures, and developed skills that complemented what he had learned during his childhood. Hunting and weapon handling gave way to more complex experiences involving combat, exploration, threat assessment, and resource management. In dungeons, he discovered that a poor decision did not result in a bad evaluation, but could cost him his life. This experience reinforced a characteristic that would accompany him throughout his journey: the ability to operate in hostile environments without relying exclusively on structures designed to protect him.

His performance attracted the attention of magical institutions. Although he had no prominent bloodline, financial resources, or political connections, Alden demonstrated combat experience and survival skills that many young mages had yet to develop. There was, therefore, a practical reason to admit him to the Academia Arcana Militaris Imperialis, one of Veridion's oldest institutions for magical training. The name preserved the memory of the former Empire of Aurelion, which had disappeared centuries earlier and been replaced by independent arcane city-states. Despite its imperial designation, the academy maintained an essentially practical mission, training mages to serve in defense forces, accompany expeditions, and confront threats in regions far from urban centers.

Alden was admitted through the positions reserved for candidates with potential for field service. The opportunity seemed to offer the possibility of building a career and giving his family a future different from the one into which he had been born. However, he soon realized that being admitted to the academy did not mean being recognized as a legitimate member of that world. He was still the boy from the countryside, without an important surname or family tradition capable of opening doors. Some classmates called him an Ascendant, apparently as a way of acknowledging his journey, but he discovered that when he was absent, they referred to him as a social climber.

The experience reinforced his determination to earn recognition, but it also deepened his understanding of the limitations imposed by social origins. Alden did not simply want to belong to the magical elite. He wanted to demonstrate that he could achieve the same results as those who had enjoyed every advantage since childhood. At that point, his motivation was still centered on personal achievement and the possibility of proving his own worth. Revenge was not his objective, although the resentment he had accumulated was already part of how he interpreted power relationships.

## The Creature and the Pact

During a break in his training, Alden began to question whether the academy truly represented the path he wanted to follow. The institution offered access to the knowledge and opportunities he sought, but it also brought him closer to a society whose rules he continued to struggle to accept. He missed the roads and the freedom he found away from institutional structures. In this context, he decided to explore a particularly difficult dungeon, known not only for the presence of dangerous creatures but also for the interference it exerted on magic.

As he advanced, Alden realized that his ability to channel energy was progressively diminishing. For other adventurers, this condition would make the expedition practically unfeasible. He, however, had enough experience to rely on his bow, spear, and survival skills developed before his magic awakened. His independence from spells allowed him to advance much further than other groups had managed.

At the end of the dungeon, he encountered a creature that did not belong to that world. Its presence interfered with the manifestation of magic around it, draining the energy of mages and keeping the creature itself alive. Alden realized he was not facing an ordinary beast. The creature was weakened and seemed as displaced from its surroundings as he himself had often felt in Veridion. It was the creature that initiated the conversation, explaining that it had crossed a boundary between realities and become trapped in that world, with no certainty that it would ever return.

To survive, it needed to consume a specific form of energy produced by the combination of magical nature and intense emotional states. Mages possessed the necessary nature but did not produce that energy in sufficient quantities. Non-mages carried the necessary emotions but lacked the compatible magical nature. Alden possessed both conditions. He had been born without apparent magic, spent his childhood being treated as inferior, and developed deep resentment toward those who concentrated power. The creature recognized him as a compatible source and made no attempt to conceal its interest in his existence.

The proposal involved an exchange. The creature needed Alden to continue existing and would offer him, in return, access to anti-magic, an ability associated with an ancient bloodline that scholars considered to have disappeared centuries earlier. Anti-magic was not simply a more powerful form of spellcasting. Its fundamental characteristic was to interrupt the connection between a magical action and the traces that would normally make it possible to identify the caster. A spell would continue to produce its effects, but its signature would no longer provide conventional investigators with the elements needed to connect the event to the person responsible.

Alden immediately understood the significance of that ability. Anti-magic offered more than an advantage in combat or a new way to explore dungeons. It allowed him to act outside one of the main control mechanisms of magical society. He knew that pacts could produce consequences extending beyond the initial intentions of those involved and that there was no guarantee of trust between him and the creature. Even so, he recognized an opportunity he might never encounter again. The creature needed him, and he needed it to understand and control an ability that could completely change his position in that world.

Alden believed in the Creator, but he did not know whether there was any will behind that sequence of events. He could not determine whether the encounter was a matter of chance or whether there was some purpose he could not yet understand. This uncertainty did not resolve his decision. Faced with a concrete opportunity and fully aware of the risks involved, he accepted the pact.

## The Secret of the Anti-Mages

After leaving the dungeon, Alden returned to the academy and began discreetly researching anti-magic. He needed to understand the nature of the power he had received and discover why such a significant ability had practically disappeared from historical records. He consulted magical treatises, ancient documents, and studies on the investigation of magical crimes. References were scarce and frequently treated the subject as superstition or associated its existence with a bloodline that had disappeared centuries earlier.

His research led him to an old book whose pages were worn and whose margins contained partially erased notes. The document recorded events that magical institutions seemed to prefer forgetting. It was there that Alden discovered that anti-mages were not a legend. They had existed and had been exterminated over generations. Anyone identified as possessing that ability was considered a threat that the institutions could not tolerate.

The reason was directly related to the nature of anti-magic. Conventional investigative methods depended largely on identifying magical signatures left behind by spells. Investigators could analyze changes in the environment, follow traces of energy, and reconstruct events to establish a connection between cause and effect. Anti-magic disrupted precisely that connection. The fire continued to burn, a door remained open, an object disappeared, and a person remained dead, but the magical connection between the event and the person responsible ceased to exist. There was no signature to follow and no magical trace capable of identifying the perpetrator.

Alden understood that the problem was not merely the offensive potential of anti-mages, but the impossibility of subjecting them to the same mechanisms of investigation and accountability applied to everyone else. An anti-mage could be honest or criminal, but the institutions had no reliable means of distinguishing between the two. The ability to act without leaving traces represented a direct threat to the control structure of magical society. The extermination of an entire bloodline remained an atrocity, but the institutional fear that had motivated the persecution was not entirely unfounded.

The discovery changed Alden's perception of the pact itself. Until then, he had considered anti-magic an opportunity for independence and an advantage over those who had been born into superior positions. Now he understood that his very existence could be enough to condemn him to death. Even without having committed any crime, he would be hunted if his secret were discovered. The problem was no longer simply learning to control the ability, but ensuring that no one knew he possessed it.

This conclusion determined his next decision. Anti-magic would not be discovered because Alden lost a battle, but because someone investigated an event and realized that something did not make sense. To avoid that, he needed to understand the methods investigators used, comprehend legal procedures, identify which forms of evidence could replace magical traces, and discover how authorities formulated hypotheses, eliminated suspects, and reconstructed sequences of events. His military training would not be enough. Alden needed to change careers and become an investigator.

At that point, the decision did not represent a vocation or a commitment to justice. It was a survival strategy. To protect his secret, he needed to understand those who might come looking for him and the limitations of the mechanisms they used. There was, however, a consequence he had not yet fully considered: learning how an investigation worked also meant learning where it could fail. The ability to interrupt magical traces gave him a way to act without being identified. Investigative knowledge could allow him to understand how to direct suspicion elsewhere.

## The Investigator

Changing careers required Alden to convince his mentors that his experience as an adventurer could be put to use in field investigations. The proposal met with resistance, but there was also institutional interest in his ability to gather information about Veridion's enemies. His mentors realized that evidence obtained by an investigator with field experience could be used to pressure adversaries, negotiate agreements, and even create opportunities for blackmail. Alden considered this purpose despicable, but accepted the conditions because he needed to remain close to investigations.

Over the following years, he received training in crime scene analysis, interpretation of magical evidence, interrogations, reconstruction of events, and legal procedures. He also studied institutional hierarchies and the limits of investigative authority. Anti-magic remained a secret, but Alden already understood that its usefulness would depend on combining supernatural ability with knowledge of conventional procedures. He did not intend to rely on a single power instead of his training. He needed to develop enough competence for his results to be recognized regardless of how he had obtained certain information.

His first significant opportunity arose when a necromancer sect was discovered. His supervisors preferred to assign the case to more experienced investigators, considering Alden too young to take the lead. He insisted and managed to lead the investigation. Anti-magic allowed him to access certain protected locations without leaving signatures that would reveal his presence, while his previous experience provided the resources needed to assess hostile environments and act cautiously. The combination of these abilities allowed him to identify the members of the sect, understand its operation, recover dangerous artifacts, and gather enough evidence to dismantle it.

The result forced his superiors to acknowledge his competence. Alden received a commendation and began to be regarded as a promising investigator. For the first time, he felt that the different stages of his training were converging into an activity in which his experiences made sense. His knowledge of the countryside, the skills acquired on the roads, his mastery of magic, and his understanding of investigative methods were no longer isolated competencies, but had become a consistent professional advantage.

More investigations followed, along with greater responsibilities and opportunities to access people and institutions that had previously been beyond his reach. His reputation grew until he was granted a seat as an observer on the Arcane Council. He did not yet have the authority to participate directly in decisions, but he could follow discussions, understand the arguments being used, and observe firsthand those who determined Veridion's direction. For Alden, the invitation represented more than professional recognition. It was an opportunity to understand the structure he had spent his life observing from below. No one in that environment knew, however, that his proximity to power also served a purpose related to protecting his own secret.

## The Arcane Council

The Arcane Council was one of the main guarantees of stability in Veridion. It brought together some of the most experienced and powerful mages in the city-state, responsible for protecting the population, preserving order, and arbitrating conflicts between factions. To society, its members represented the authority necessary to maintain political and magical balance. Alden, however, began to realize that the institution's legitimacy did not prevent its members from using its own rules to protect private interests.

One of the men who attracted his attention most was Aurelius Aurelian Septimus, the Council's president. Alden had known him since childhood. Aurelius had maintained ties with his family and frequently mistreated them, making it clear that he considered people of similar origins inferior. Institutional proximity allowed Alden to observe this behavior from another perspective and understand how Aurelius's authority manifested itself behind the scenes.

It was in this environment that he learned about the situation involving Tiberian Valerian, a publicly respected Archmage who was nevertheless associated with smuggling, extortion, and activities conducted by criminal factions. There were investigators who believed they had enough evidence to justify a formal investigation and even his preventive removal. The process, however, was going nowhere because Tiberian had Aurelius under his influence.

The difficulty did not stem solely from a personal relationship involving corruption. The Council's own rules established that an Archmage's removal had to be formally placed on the agenda by the president before the other members could deliberate on the matter. The rule served a legitimate purpose, as it prevented internal disputes and political rivalries from being used to remove members without an institutional process. In this case, however, the protection intended to prevent abuses had become a mechanism for blocking action. As long as Aurelius refused to allow the matter to be discussed, the Council could not even formally deliberate on Tiberian's situation.

What bothered Alden most was realizing that the situation was no secret. Officials, investigators, and members of different departments knew about the problems, but many preferred to treat them as inevitable consequences of politics. Corruption was taking place within a legitimate institution, in front of people who understood its rules and knew how to use them to prevent its members from being held accountable. Even so, to the population, those men continued to be the city's guardians.

Alden began to realize that an institution did not need to be entirely corrupt to produce unjust outcomes. Many Council members were genuinely committed to protecting Veridion and believed that the rules existed to prevent abuses. The problem was that the same structure that limited the arbitrary exercise of power could also be exploited by those with enough influence to control its application. Injustice did not necessarily depend on breaking the rules. Under certain circumstances, it was enough to control who had the authority to invoke them.

The discovery represented an important shift in how Alden interpreted his own position. During childhood, he had experienced the effects of power from the perspective of someone subjected to it. Now he was beginning to understand how that power protected itself internally, which mechanisms it used to preserve its members, and why an investigator's competence was not always enough to deliver justice. Proximity to the Council had not resolved his distrust. It had provided concrete grounds for deepening it.

## The Pyromancer Vigilante

The situation involving Tiberian and Aurelius placed Alden before an opportunity that combined his indignation with the resentment he had accumulated since childhood. For years, he had viewed Aurelius as one of the clearest representatives of the elite that had subjected his family to unequal conditions. Now he discovered that the same man used his position to protect an Archmage associated with criminal organizations. Within the Council's rules, Alden believed he would hardly be able to bring about any change. Outside them, however, he possessed an ability that virtually no authority could trace.

His first conclusion was that he needed to target Aurelius's wealth. He believed that putting financial pressure on the president would be more effective than confronting him directly or exposing his own existence as an anti-mage. He began with a remote property, using anti-magic to bypass its protections and set it on fire. The flames, however, were enhanced with mana, making them more intense and difficult to control. Alden checked beforehand whether the properties were empty and tried to ensure that no one would be harmed. His stated objective was to destroy Aurelius's wealth, not target his family.

The actions were repeated across different properties and businesses. Warehouses were set ablaze, buildings were destroyed, and losses began to accumulate. The absence of magical traces prevented investigators from connecting the attacks to a perpetrator, while the nature of the targets made the events appear to be part of disputes between arcane families. Alden believed he had found a way to pressure the president without compromising his identity. The problem was that Aurelius continued to resist and refused to put Tiberian's removal on the agenda.

The repeated incursions began to consume time and energy. Alden needed to study routines, choose targets, bypass protections, and avoid any pattern that might raise suspicion. With each new action, the risk increased without producing the expected result. It was in this context that he began considering targeting Aurelius's family's main property. He knew the decision was different from the previous ones, since the president's family members had not necessarily participated in the corruption or prevented the Council from acting. Even so, he constructed a justification based on the privileges they enjoyed and the benefits they received from Aurelius's position.

Alden decided to cause enough fear to demonstrate that the president's resistance would have consequences. The property was attacked, and the people were evacuated in time, but panic spread among family members, guards, and allies. For the first time, Alden clearly realized that he had used the fear of people who were not necessarily guilty as a means of pressure. Remorse came immediately. For a while, his justification seemed to lose all its force, because there was no blocked investigation or protected criminal involved, but rather a family that had just discovered someone could bypass its defenses and set its property on fire.

Alden could have stopped at that moment, but decided to continue waiting for the outcome. At the next meeting, Aurelius finally placed Tiberian's removal on the Council's agenda. Alden interpreted the event as a victory. He was exhausted but satisfied, believing he could let the institutional process take its course. The pressure had produced the result he wanted, and he would not need to continue acting in the shadows.

The following day, however, Aurelius approached him personally. The president greeted him cordially, acknowledged his reputation, and showed interest in his professional journey. Alden was unsettled when he realized that the man he had turned into an almost monstrous figure did not entirely match the image he had carried since childhood. Aurelius remained someone who had protected Tiberian and used his authority to prevent the Council from acting, but he was also a man capable of showing genuine concern for his own family and cordiality toward a promising young investigator. The contrast made it even harder for Alden to deal with what he had done.

Aurelius explained that he had begun to suspect an anti-mage was operating in Veridion and wanted Alden to lead the investigation. The reputation he had earned in the necromancer sect case had convinced the president that he possessed the necessary competence. The invitation immediately triggered fear. Alden did not know whether he had been discovered or whether the conversation was an attempt to test his reaction. The man whose family he had just targeted was now placing him at the center of the investigation that could reveal his greatest secret. Despite his apprehension, he accepted the assignment.

## The Investigator of His Own Crimes

Alden soon understood that Aurelius had no idea who he was looking for. The president's suspicion was not a disguised accusation, but an opportunity for Alden to learn the methods that could be used against him. The assignment allowed him to closely monitor the investigation into anti-magic while also observing individuals who would hardly be investigated through other means. His official position became cover for increasingly extensive clandestine activities.

At night, Alden began breaking into the properties of suspected mages. The residences of the most powerful were protected by barriers, runes, alarms, and detection mechanisms, but anti-magic allowed him to bypass these defenses without producing the signature that would normally reveal an intrusion. Inside the properties, he searched for correspondence, documents, financial records, and other evidence capable of exposing illicit activities. It did not take long for him to discover that corruption in Veridion was more widespread than he had imagined. Archmages were involved in smuggling, extortion, and clandestine agreements, while influential families protected criminal operations and used their political relationships to obstruct investigations.

Alden's advantage was not limited to accessing protected locations. During the day, he could use his position as an investigator to turn illegally obtained information into cases conducted through official mechanisms. An apparently casual observation led to a question, which justified a new line of inquiry and made it possible to request documents. Those documents revealed other connections until the investigation reached people and operations Alden already knew from the previous night's incursions. To his colleagues, his ability to identify connections seemed exceptional. In practice, while others were trying to discover what had happened, he had already visited the locations and examined the evidence before any formal investigation began.

This method produced concrete results. Alden managed to gather evidence against corrupt Archmages who had evaded accountability for years. In some cases, the institutions finally managed to act because he had found the missing elements. His reputation grew, as did the trust placed in him by his superiors and the Council. The competence demonstrated in official investigations expanded his access, while anti-magic provided information that no other investigator could obtain in the same way. The success of each operation reinforced his position and created the conditions for him to conduct even more complex investigations.

The combination, however, also changed his relationship with authority. Alden had begun investigating to protect his own identity, but now he used his knowledge to circumvent institutional limits. He no longer restricted himself to discovering crimes and handing evidence over to the authorities. He began deciding which information would be revealed, which investigations should advance, and how official mechanisms could be directed toward the results he considered appropriate. His position as an investigator, originally a survival strategy, was becoming an instrument of influence over the system itself.

## When the Guilty Cease to Be the Target

Alden's first targets were genuinely criminals. They were men who commanded illicit organizations, exploited non-mages, trafficked people, and used money and influence to escape the consequences of their actions. When official investigations failed to hold them accountable, Alden once again turned to the shadows. He believed that the absence of institutional responses justified his intervention and that the concrete results demonstrated the legitimacy of his choices. Criminal networks were dismantled, people were saved, and operations that had survived for years ceased to exist.

The problem was that corruption continued to find ways to protect some of its members. Evidence disappeared, witnesses changed their statements, and investigations were halted before reaching those responsible. Investigators received orders to abandon certain lines of inquiry or were transferred to other cases. Alden began to realize that it was not necessary to control an entire institution to prevent it from acting. It was enough to influence the right people, pressure those responsible for certain decisions, and compromise the elements supporting an investigation.

It was in this context that he began to kill. At first, he chose individuals he considered responsible for serious crimes and who, in his assessment, would never be properly punished through conventional mechanisms. The deaths were planned, and Alden found justifications for each of them. He believed that the concrete results compensated for violating procedures and that someone needed to take responsibility for decisions the institutions avoided making. His work as an investigator and his clandestine identity began to merge. Investigation no longer served only to determine who was guilty, but also to decide who should receive a punishment Alden considered necessary.

The change became evident when an investigator close to him began noticing inconsistencies in his conduct. He had known Alden long enough to realize that some investigations advanced with unusual precision and that Alden often seemed to know where to look before anyone else. Without sufficient evidence to make an accusation, he began asking questions. Alden tried to deflect suspicion, alter the course of certain inquiries, and present explanations that would preserve his position. He considered alternatives that did not involve violence, such as discrediting his colleague, arranging a transfer, or temporarily suspending his clandestine activities. None of them, however, seemed to eliminate the risk.

Alden decided to kill the investigator. This time, there was no criminal organization behind the target, no corruption to fight, and no victims waiting for justice. The man represented only a threat to the possibility of Alden's secret being discovered. After the murder, Alden tried to use the same justifications he had employed before, telling himself that he needed to protect his identity and prevent everything he had built from being destroyed. However, the explanation no longer held together as easily. For the first time, he had killed someone who did not need to be punished, but merely prevented from discovering the truth.

The decision revealed a transformation Alden could not yet define. He did not know whether it had begun when he set fire to Aurelius's first property, when he felt satisfaction at striking someone he considered untouchable, or in the later killings, when he began believing he could produce better results than the institutions themselves. He only knew that he had crossed an important boundary. For a long time, he had justified his actions through the corruption in Veridion and the system's inability to hold its members accountable. Now he was beginning to realize that he might not be acting only because he considered it necessary. He might also be acting because he wanted the power to decide who should be punished.

## The Anti-Mage Archmage

While Alden pursued his private war against corruption, Veridion began facing consequences that extended beyond the individuals targeted by his actions. Political disputes intensified, trust in institutions declined, and the Council's different factions increasingly saw one another as growing threats. At the same time, other magical societies realized that Veridion's cities were becoming more vulnerable. The Archmages Alden eliminated were not merely politicians or men protected by privilege. They also occupied important positions in the defense of the cities and held knowledge accumulated over decades of experience.

Removing a corrupt Archmage could represent a victory for his victims while simultaneously creating a problem for collective security. Each death left a position that needed to be filled, not always by someone with equivalent experience. Alden was beginning to understand that justice applied to an individual could not be evaluated exclusively by the immediate result of that individual's punishment. A man could deserve to be held accountable for his crimes and yet his absence could produce consequences that placed other people in danger. The individual logic guiding his crusade did not adequately account for society's dependence on certain positions and competencies.

The city began to feel the effects of these absences precisely when it needed to recover its stability. External threats increased, internal disputes became more aggressive, and the Council's mechanisms of balance struggled to respond to the situation. Alden had not intended to provoke a war between magical societies, but his actions were helping create an environment in which conflict became more likely. For the first time, the consequences of his actions extended beyond the individuals he considered guilty and affected the very structure that was supposed to protect the population.

It was in this context that Aurelius decided to appoint him Archmage. The choice seemed natural to those who had followed his career. Alden had built an exceptional reputation as an investigator, possessed military and field experience, had faced significant threats, and earned recognition without depending on the old magical families. In a society increasingly distrustful of its institutions, he represented a convenient alternative: someone apparently competent, disciplined, and incorruptible, whose rise was not associated with traditional power groups.

Aurelius, in particular, trusted Alden. He knew his results, had followed his professional development, and believed his ability to identify corruption could help the Council recover some of its authority. He did not know that a significant part of this competence had been built on information obtained illegally during nighttime incursions, nor that the investigator he considered an example of integrity had used his position to conceal his own crimes. The appointment placed Alden exactly at the center of the structure he had spent his life questioning. The boy from the countryside now held a position among Veridion's most powerful men.

Faced with this new responsibility, Alden decided to suspend his clandestine activities, at least temporarily. The decision did not represent a full acknowledgment of his mistakes or remorse for his previous actions. For the first time, he possessed enough legitimate authority, resources, and influence to attempt to change the institutions without relying exclusively on anti-magic. Perhaps it was possible to protect non-mages, reduce the scope of corruption, and limit the privileges of the old families through changes implemented from within the system itself. The city's instability and the growing external threats made this alternative even more relevant.

The possibility of reforming the institutions represented an opportunity Alden had not had at any previous stage. He no longer needed to break into properties to discover crimes or decide on his own when someone should die. He could use his position to pressure the Council, influence decisions, and build legitimate changes. However, this opportunity also required him to acknowledge the limits of his own authority. The man who had learned to act without leaving traces and without answering to anyone would need to accept that the legitimate exercise of power depends on procedures, oversight, and accountability.

Anti-magic remained his greatest secret, and his past could not simply be erased by his appointment. Alden knew he had manipulated investigations, used information obtained clandestinely to direct official proceedings, and committed murders that could never be justified by the principles he had initially defended. Some of his victims were criminals; others had become targets because they represented threats to his identity. At some point, the distinction between fighting corruption and protecting himself had ceased to guide his decisions with the clarity he himself believed he possessed.

To society, however, Alden was the man who had overcome the limitations of his origins and earned a position of authority through merit. Aurelius saw him as a trusted ally. The Council regarded him as a promising Archmage, capable of contributing to Veridion's institutional recovery. Few could imagine that his journey had also been built upon actions that escaped the control mechanisms he was now expected to preserve.

His appointment marks the end of one stage of Alden's life, but it does not resolve the contradiction at the heart of his story. He finally possesses enough power to confront the structures he considers unjust, but must decide whether he can exercise that authority without reproducing the same behavior he once condemned. Throughout his life, he believed the problem lay with those who could make decisions about others without being held accountable. Now he occupies a position in which he could act in precisely that way.

The question is no longer simply what Alden will do with the power he has acquired, but whether he will be able to accept the limits necessary to exercise it. Anti-magic remains within him, as does the conviction that someone must act when institutions fail. What remains to be seen is whether Alden will be capable of building a structure in which no one needs to stand above the rules or whether, when faced with the first difficulties, he will once again believe that only he has the competence to decide when they should be ignored.

The man who spent his life fighting impunity has reached the top. Now he must discover whether he can answer for his own decisions.
