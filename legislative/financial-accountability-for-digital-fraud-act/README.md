# Financial Accountability for Digital Fraud Act

## 1. Concept

Establish a law to strengthen the financial accountability of individuals and legal entities involved in digital fraud, ensuring that those responsible face economic consequences greater than the benefits obtained from the crime.

The proposal is not limited to increasing prison sentences. Its central principle is to ensure that fraudsters fully repay the amounts stolen and pay a punitive fine to the State, making the activity economically disadvantageous.

The law must establish the following mechanisms:

1. Full restitution to victims

- Repayment of 100% of the amount stolen or fraudulently obtained.
    
- Monetary adjustment from the date the loss occurred.
    
- Interest charged according to applicable legal criteria.
    
- Liability for reimbursement even when the amounts have been transferred, divided, or concealed through third parties.
    
- Amounts allocated to restitution must be paid to the respective victims and must not be conflated with fine revenue.
    

2. Punitive fine of five times the amount of the illicit benefit

In addition to full restitution to victims, those responsible must pay the Federal Government a fine equivalent to five times the economic benefit obtained through the fraud.

This fine will be punitive, not compensatory, in nature. Payment of the fine will neither replace nor reduce the obligation to compensate victims.

The penalty will not depend on the responsible party's revenue, declared assets, or economic size.

Mandatory allocation of proceeds:

- 100% of the amounts collected from these fines will be allocated to the Federal Government's Treasury.
    
- The funds will be exclusively allocated to paying down federal public debt, including the amortization and settlement of obligations.
    
- The amounts must be accounted for separately, ensuring transparency regarding the funds collected and effectively applied toward reducing public debt.
    
- The proceeds may not be used to finance administrative or ordinary government expenses.
    

Example: in a fraud involving an illicit benefit of R$ 10,000:

- R$ 10,000 in restitution to the victim.
    
- R$ 50,000 in punitive fines allocated to the Federal Government.
    
- The entire R$ 50,000 will be directed toward payment of federal public debt.
    
- Total amount owed by the responsible party: R$ 60,000, before applicable monetary adjustments and interest.
    

3. Accountability of intermediaries and receivers of stolen funds

Anyone who knowingly participates in the movement, receipt, transfer, concealment, or use of funds obtained through digital fraud must also be held liable.

The proposal must distinguish between two situations:

- Participation in the fraud: anyone who provides accounts, receives or moves funds as part of the scheme, knowing its criminal purpose, may be held liable for the fraud itself, subject to the same penalties applicable to the other participants and taking their culpability into account.
    
- Subsequent receipt of funds: anyone who receives, acquires, holds, or conceals funds knowing their criminal origin, even without having participated in the original fraud, must be held liable under a specific offense of receiving proceeds of digital fraud, with criminal penalties equivalent to those established for the fraud that generated the funds.
    

Liability must require proof of knowledge of the illicit origin and of the conduct involved; merely receiving a transfer will not be sufficient.

4. Joint and several liability

Participants proven to be involved in the same scheme may be held jointly and severally liable for restitution and the resulting financial obligations, while preserving the individualization of criminal liability.

The total amount collected may not exceed the amount owed, preventing victims from receiving multiple payments for the same loss.

The punitive fine will be imposed on those responsible according to their participation and liability as established in the proceedings, without duplicating the penalty for the same conduct.

5. Repeat offenses and serial fraud

Repeated digital fraud must constitute a specific aggravating circumstance.

Previous final convictions for digital fraud or the specific offense of receiving proceeds of digital fraud must be considered when determining recidivism, in accordance with applicable legal rules.

The use of multiple victims, bank accounts, identities, or transactions to carry out successive frauds must be considered when assessing whether serial fraud has occurred.

## 2. Rationale

Digital fraud has become a criminal activity capable of affecting thousands of people, often using organized structures, third-party accounts, shell companies, and mechanisms for rapidly transferring funds.

The accountability system must target not only those who devise the fraud but also those who enable its execution and those who accept or conceal the proceeds.

Brazilian legislation already provides penalties for electronic fraud, crimes involving virtual assets, receiving stolen goods, and providing bank accounts for the circulation of criminal proceeds. However, this proposal seeks to strengthen financial recovery for victims and establish economic consequences proportionate to the illicit benefit.

The goal is to eliminate the economic appeal of crime: those responsible must return what they stole, compensate for the losses, and pay a punitive fine to the Federal Government.

In addition to its punitive function, the fine will have a specific fiscal purpose: generating additional federal government revenue, allocated exclusively to payment of public debt. In this way, accountability for fraud will also contribute to reducing the country's debt burden.

## 3. Expected benefits

- Increase the recovery of amounts lost by victims.
    
- Make digital fraud economically disadvantageous.
    
- Discourage the use of third-party accounts to move illicit funds.
    
- Hold knowingly involved intermediaries and receivers of stolen funds accountable.
    
- Reduce the appeal of criminal structures based on the fragmentation and concealment of funds.
    
- Increase the effectiveness of accountability for criminal organizations.
    
- Establish harsher consequences for repeat offenders.
    
- Increase Federal Government revenue through punitive fines imposed on those responsible.
    
- Generate an additional source of funds for the amortization and payment of federal public debt.
    
- Contribute to reducing public debt without imposing new taxes on the population.
    

## 4. Potential impacts

Positive impacts:

- Greater potential for asset recovery.
    
- Reduced expected profitability of fraudulent schemes.
    
- Greater accountability for secondary participants.
    
- Incentives for cooperation among financial institutions, law enforcement authorities, and investigative agencies.
    
- Greater financial protection for victims.
    
- Increased Federal Government revenue.
    
- Direct contribution to the amortization of federal public debt.
    
- Greater transparency regarding the collection and allocation of fines.
    

Operational and legal impacts:

- Need to improve mechanisms for tracing and freezing funds.
    
- Need to identify the ultimate beneficiaries of transfers and transactions.
    
- Increased demand for financial investigations and analysis of suspicious transactions.
    
- Need for mechanisms to prevent double collection for the same loss.
    
- Need to ensure proportionality, individualization of liability, and the right to a defense.
    
- Creation of a specific accounting mechanism to identify and track amounts collected and allocated to public debt.
    

## 5. Feasibility and challenges

The proposal must complement existing criminal and financial legislation, avoiding the duplication of offenses or incompatible penalties.

Law No. 15,397/2026 has already introduced significant amendments to the Criminal Code, including increased penalties for fraud and receiving stolen goods, as well as the criminalization of providing bank accounts for the circulation of criminal proceeds.

Therefore, the new law should focus primarily on financial accountability, asset recovery, and the specific treatment of individuals involved in digital fraud.

The following criteria must guide its implementation:

- Calculation basis: the amount of the illicit economic benefit actually obtained, without automatically using total transaction volume as a substitute.
    
- Restitution: full recovery of the victim's proven loss.
    
- Fine: five times the amount of the illicit benefit, allocated entirely to the Federal Government, in addition to restitution.
    
- Allocation: exclusive use of amounts collected through fines for payment of federal public debt.
    
- Transparency: periodic publication of amounts collected, transferred, and effectively used for the amortization or settlement of debt.
    
- Financial independence: the penalty may not be reduced simply because the responsible party has limited assets or low revenue, subject to applicable enforcement mechanisms.
    
- Joint and several liability: participants in the same scheme may be held jointly and severally liable for financial obligations, without duplicating the total amount owed.
    
- Tracing: possibility of asset investigations and judicial freezing of funds and assets related to the fraud.
    
- Third parties: accountability of intermediaries and receivers of stolen funds, subject to proof of knowledge and knowing participation.
    
- Recidivism: specific aggravation for previous final convictions for digital fraud or related offenses involving the receipt of stolen funds.
    
- Protection of rights: protection of bona fide third parties who had no knowledge of or participation in the criminal activity.
    

## 6. Open issues

- Should the fivefold penalty be calculated based on the amount obtained by the criminal or on the total loss caused to victims when these amounts differ?
    
- How should cases be handled in which the money is recovered before the proceedings are concluded?
    
- Should financial liability extend to companies knowingly used to carry out or conceal fraud?
    
- How should the liability of institutions that have failed to comply with mandatory prevention controls be determined?
    
- How should fraud committed by criminal organizations operating internationally be handled?
    
- How should recovered amounts be distributed when there are multiple victims?
    
- How can it be ensured that collected amounts are effectively allocated to federal public debt without being diverted to other expenses?
    
- How can the new offense of receiving proceeds of digital fraud be reconciled with the offense of receiving stolen goods already provided for in the Criminal Code?
