# National Supply Resilience Policy

## 1. Idea

Establish a national policy aimed at reducing Brazil's vulnerability to external supply shocks, ensuring that international crises, geopolitical conflicts, pandemics, natural disasters, or global logistics disruptions do not cause shortages of essential products within the national territory.

The policy's primary instrument would be the development and maintenance of domestic production capacity for goods considered critical. The origin of capital or the nationality of companies would be irrelevant: Brazilian and foreign companies could participate equally, provided they maintain actual production operations in the country.

The objective would not be to achieve self-sufficiency, replace all imports, or artificially protect domestic producers, but to ensure that Brazil has sufficient domestic supply alternatives to withstand significant disruptions in international trade.

The policy could combine temporary incentives for establishing and expanding production facilities, maintaining strategic production capacity, building safety stocks, and diversifying suppliers and logistics routes.

The selection of priority products would consider their importance to the functioning of the economy and society, the geographic concentration of global production, Brazil's dependence on imports, the time required to replace suppliers, and the possibility of supply disruptions.

## 2. Motivation

International economic integration allows countries to access products at lower costs and with greater variety. However, it also creates vulnerabilities when the supply of certain goods depends excessively on a limited number of countries, manufacturers, or logistics routes.

Recent and historical crises demonstrate that international trade can experience significant disruptions during periods of geopolitical instability, military conflicts, pandemics, natural disasters, or export restrictions. Under such circumstances, even countries with the financial capacity to import may face difficulties acquiring certain products, either because of global unavailability or because producing countries prioritize supplying their domestic markets.

Dependence on imports is not necessarily a problem under normal market conditions. However, it becomes a risk when the interruption of certain products' supply can compromise essential services, productive activities, agricultural production, infrastructure, or the functioning of entire supply chains.

In this context, economic policy should not assess the efficiency of a supply chain exclusively based on acquisition costs under normal conditions. It should also consider the economic and social costs of a potential disruption.

Maintaining domestic production capacity can serve as a form of economic insurance: it may represent an additional cost under normal conditions, but it reduces the country's exposure to external supply crises.

## 3. Expected Benefits

- Greater supply security: reduced risk of shortages of essential products during disruptions to international trade.
- Economic resilience: reduced vulnerability of productive sectors to external supply shocks.
- Continuity of essential services: greater ability to maintain the operation of healthcare, energy, telecommunications, transportation, agriculture, and infrastructure systems.
- Attraction of investment: incentives for establishing production facilities in Brazil by both domestic and foreign companies.
- Production diversification: reduced dependence on a limited number of international suppliers and expansion of domestic supply alternatives.
- Technological and industrial development: expansion of production capabilities, engineering, research, and development in sectors considered critical.
- Greater crisis response capacity: ability to rapidly expand domestic production when international suppliers face restrictions.
- Reduced economic losses: lower exposure to industrial shutdowns, service disruptions, and sudden price increases caused by shortages.

## 4. Potential Impacts

The implementation of the policy could stimulate investment in sectors where external dependence represents a significant risk of supply disruption.

Potential priority sectors include:

- Healthcare: active pharmaceutical ingredients, essential medicines, medical equipment, and hospital supplies.
- Agriculture: fertilizers, crop protection products, and machinery and equipment components.
- Technology: semiconductors, electronic components, telecommunications equipment, and information technology infrastructure.
- Energy: batteries, energy storage systems, electrical equipment, and components for power generation and distribution.
- Industry: machinery, industrial components, specialty materials, and inputs that are difficult to replace.
- Infrastructure: equipment and components required for the operation of transportation, sanitation, and telecommunications systems.

The economic impacts would depend on the structure of each sector. In certain cases, establishing domestic production capacity could increase costs compared with imports under normal conditions. In others, it could increase competition, attract investment, and generate productivity gains.

It would also be possible to maintain partially idle production capacity in highly critical sectors, provided that the cost of this additional capacity is lower than the potential losses resulting from a supply disruption.

The policy could also require the maintenance of strategic stocks for products whose production cannot be rapidly expanded or for which establishing domestic production capacity is economically unfeasible.

## 5. Feasibility and Challenges

Implementation would require the establishment of objective criteria to identify products and sectors whose external dependence represents significant risks to the country.

Prioritization should consider, among other factors:

- Brazil's degree of dependence on imports.
- Geographic concentration of global production.
- Number and diversity of available suppliers.
- Importance of the product to essential services and supply chains.
- Time required to find alternative suppliers.
- Time required to establish or expand domestic production.
- Cost of maintaining domestic production capacity.
- Possibility of replacing the product with available alternatives.

Public incentives could include temporary tax benefits, long-term financing, support for establishing industrial facilities, and mechanisms for contracting strategic production capacity.

These incentives should be conditional on the effective establishment and maintenance of production capacity in Brazil, with verifiable targets for investment, production, availability, and operational performance.

The policy should also allow foreign companies to participate without requiring domestic ownership or mandatory transfers of intellectual property. The central requirement would be the existence of actual production capacity within Brazilian territory.

An important challenge would be avoiding economically unjustifiable investments. Not every imported product needs to be manufactured domestically, and not every external dependency represents a significant risk. The policy should concentrate resources on products whose disruption could cause significant harm and for which a technically viable domestic solution exists.

It would also be necessary to prevent temporary incentives from becoming permanent subsidies to companies that are unable to operate competitively. Supply capacity should be periodically assessed, allowing priorities to be reviewed and incentives to be discontinued when they are no longer necessary.

## 6. Open Questions

- What criteria would formally define a product as critical to national supply?
- What percentage of domestic demand should be met through domestic production in each sector?
- Under what circumstances would it be preferable to maintain domestic production capacity, strategic stocks, or diversify international suppliers?
- How should the economic value of production capacity maintained as protection against future crises be calculated?
- Which sectors should receive initial priority?
- How should incentives be structured to ensure that companies maintain available production capacity even when domestic production is not the lowest-cost alternative?
- How should supply risks be periodically assessed and the list of priority products updated?
- How should the policy be coordinated with foreign trade, industrial development, infrastructure, and economic security strategies?
