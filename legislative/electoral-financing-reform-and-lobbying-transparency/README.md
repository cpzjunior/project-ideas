# Electoral Financing Reform and Lobbying Transparency

## 1. Proposal

The proposal seeks to improve the financing of electoral campaigns, reduce the concentration of public expenditure in election years, and increase transparency in the relationships between public officials and private interest representatives.

Three complementary measures are proposed:

### 1.1. Gradual reduction of the Special Campaign Financing Fund (FEFC)

Establish a legal cap on the FEFC, calculated as a percentage of the federal government's primary expenditures.

The initial limit would be 0.20% of the federal government's primary expenditures, with a reduction of 0.01 percentage points per election, until it reaches a limit of 0.15%.

The reduction would take place gradually, over five elections, allowing political parties and candidates to progressively adapt to the new rule.

The 0.15% rate would be maintained as a permanent limit, subject to future legislative reviews.

The objective is not to eliminate public campaign financing, but to establish a predictable limit on electoral spending, preventing disproportionate increases and contributing to fiscal discipline.

The distribution of funds among political parties must continue to follow legal, transparent, and balanced criteria.

### 1.2. Allocation of 50% of electoral funding across two fiscal years

Require the total allocation planned for each election to be divided equally between the two fiscal years involved:

- 50% in the fiscal year preceding the election year: the funds are made available in advance and kept in financial investments until they are used in the election.
- 50% in the election year itself: the funds are made available to supplement campaign financing.

This division has two main objectives:

1. Reduce the budgetary burden in the election year: half of the allocation is no longer concentrated in the fiscal year in which the election takes place, distributing the budgetary requirement across two years.
2. Generate financial returns: the funds made available in advance remain invested until they are needed, generating returns during this period.

The net returns obtained from investing the funds made available in advance must be allocated exclusively to the amortization of federal public debt.

The invested principal will remain fully reserved for electoral financing. Therefore, the returns will not be deducted from the amount allocated to campaigns and may not be used for other public expenditures.

Illustrative example:

Considering a total allocation of BRL 4 billion for a given election:

- BRL 2 billion would be made available in the preceding fiscal year and kept in financial investments.
- BRL 2 billion would be made available in the election year itself.
- The net returns generated by investing the first BRL 2 billion would be allocated exclusively to the amortization of federal public debt.

Thus, the entire BRL 4 billion would remain allocated to campaigns, but half of the allocation would be brought forward, reducing the concentration of the budgetary burden in the election year.

The regulations must establish the permitted financial instruments, investment criteria, calculation of net returns, and mechanisms for transferring these amounts to the National Treasury.

### 1.3. Regulation of lobbying activities

Establish national rules for transparency, oversight, and accountability in the representation of interests before public officials.

The regulation must include:

- Mandatory registration: identification of interest representatives, the organizations they represent, and their clients or ultimate beneficiaries.
- Registration of meetings and contacts: disclosure of dates, participants, organizations involved, public officials contacted, and topics discussed.
- Financial transparency: periodic disclosure of expenses and payments related to interest representation activities.
- Legislative traceability: registration of the participation of interest representatives in drafting bills, amendments, regulations, and other public decisions.
- Prevention of conflicts of interest: establishment of rules to prevent undue favoritism and situations involving conflicts between public and private interests.
- Oversight and penalties: designation of the bodies responsible for supervision, audits, and the application of penalties in cases of non-compliance.

The regulation must preserve the legitimate participation of civil society, representative entities, companies, and citizens in decision-making processes, without creating disproportionate obstacles to the right to express opinions and participate in political affairs.

## 2. Rationale

Public campaign financing was established as an alternative to dependence on large private donors. However, its existence does not eliminate economic influence over political and administrative decisions after elections.

Public financing also represents a significant expense for the federal government, particularly in election years, when funds are concentrated in a single fiscal year.

The proposal seeks to address these issues through three mechanisms:

- Gradually reduce the volume of public funds allocated to campaigns.
- Distribute electoral funding across two fiscal years, reducing budgetary concentration in the election year and making use of financial returns generated during the advance funding period.
- Increase transparency in relationships between public officials and private interest representatives, making it possible to identify who seeks to influence public decisions, on whose behalf, and on which matters.

The central idea is to make public electoral financing more limited and predictable, while subjecting relationships involving political and economic influence to clear transparency and oversight rules.

## 3. Expected benefits

### 3.1. Fiscal and budgetary benefits

- Gradual reduction of public funds allocated to electoral financing.
- Greater predictability regarding the FEFC spending limit.
- Distribution of the budgetary burden across two fiscal years.
- Reduced concentration of expenditures in the election year.
- Use of financial returns generated by the advance investment of part of the funds.
- Allocation of net returns exclusively to the amortization of federal public debt.

### 3.2. Political and institutional benefits

- Greater transparency regarding the activities of economic groups and organizations in the public sphere.
- Identification of the interests represented in meetings and decision-making processes.
- Greater traceability of influence exerted over legislative bills and administrative decisions.
- Fewer opportunities for non-transparent relationships between public officials and private interests.
- Strengthening of institutional integrity and oversight mechanisms.

## 4. Potential impacts

### 4.1. Positive impacts

Reduction in electoral spending: the gradual limitation of the FEFC may reduce the volume of public funds allocated to campaigns over time.

Improved budget allocation: the 50% division between the two fiscal years reduces the concentration of the allocation in the election year, allowing the budgetary burden to be distributed over the period.

Financial returns: advance investment of the funds makes it possible to generate returns while the money awaits its use. These returns may contribute, even if modestly, to the amortization of public debt.

Greater political transparency: lobbying regulation makes it possible to identify the parties involved in interest representation and monitor their participation in public decisions.

Institutional strengthening: clear transparency and oversight rules can improve the integrity of legislative and administrative processes.

### 4.2. Risks and potential negative effects

Excessive reduction in public financing: even a gradual reduction in the FEFC may disadvantage smaller parties and candidates with less capacity to raise private funds if the limits are established without impact studies.

Greater dependence on private funds: reducing public financing may increase the importance of individual donations, party structures, and other legally permitted fundraising methods.

Budgetary implementation challenges: dividing the funds across fiscal years requires alignment with the rules governing budgets, financial execution, and the federal government's cash availability.

Financial risk: investment returns depend on interest rates, investment periods, and the instruments used. There is no guarantee of a fixed return.

Opportunity cost: advance investment must be compared with the alternative of using the funds to reduce the need to issue public debt. The mechanism must demonstrate an effective fiscal benefit.

Lobbying-related bureaucracy: excessive requirements may hinder the legitimate participation of organizations, civil society groups, and citizens in the political process.

## 5. Feasibility and challenges

### 5.1. Legislative feasibility

The proposal can be implemented through amendments to electoral legislation, particularly Law No. 9,504/1997, which governs campaign financing and the FEFC.

Dividing the allocation across two fiscal years will also require an assessment of its compatibility with budgetary and financial rules, including the Federal Constitution, the Fiscal Responsibility Law, and public finance legislation.

Lobbying regulation may be addressed through a separate legislative proposal or by improving bills already under consideration in the National Congress.

### 5.2. Budgetary and financial adjustments

The following aspects will need to be established:

- How the total allocation for the election will be provided for in the budget laws of the two fiscal years.
- How the funds made available in advance will be kept available until the election year.
- Which financial instruments may be used.
- How net returns will be calculated.
- How the returns will be transferred for the amortization of public debt.
- How the full preservation of the principal allocated to campaigns will be ensured.

It will also be necessary to assess the mechanism's compatibility with the principle of annuality in public budgeting and the rules governing the management of the federal government's cash balances.

### 5.3. Constitutional and electoral compliance

Changes to electoral financing rules must comply with the principle of electoral anteriority established in Article 16 of the Federal Constitution.

The gradual reduction of the FEFC must be accompanied by objective criteria, avoiding abrupt changes that could compromise equal conditions among political parties and candidates.

### 5.4. Lobbying oversight

Regulation will require a clear definition of the bodies responsible for registration, monitoring, auditing, and the application of penalties.

It will also be necessary to integrate existing transparency mechanisms, avoiding duplicated obligations and systems.

## 6. Open issues

The following aspects must be further developed before drafting the final legislative text:

1. FEFC cap calculation basis: determine whether the percentage will be calculated based on the federal government's primary expenditures, Net Current Revenue, or another fiscal indicator.
2. Reduction percentages: assess the effects of reducing the limit by 0.01 percentage points per election, starting at 0.20% until reaching the permanent limit of 0.15%.
3. Division across fiscal years: define the legal and budgetary mechanism that will allow 50% of the allocation to be made available in the preceding fiscal year and preserved until it is used.
4. Financial investments: define the permitted instruments, prioritizing security, liquidity, and transparency.
5. Net returns: establish the calculation methodology, taking into account any costs, taxes, and operating expenses.
6. Debt amortization: define the procedure for transferring returns to the National Treasury, ensuring that they are used exclusively for the amortization of federal public debt.
7. Party funding distribution: preserve transparent and balanced criteria for distributing electoral funds.
8. Lobbying regulation: define the scope of obligations, legitimate exceptions, oversight bodies, and applicable penalties.
9. Transition: establish implementation deadlines that comply with electoral anteriority and allow the bodies involved to adapt.
